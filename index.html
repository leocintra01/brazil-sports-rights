Telecom Brazil — A more competitive first half: what it means for Vivo and TIM

Overview — what changed in recent months

Vivo and TIM shares declined following their 2Q26 results, and we have received many investor questions on what changed. For roughly four years, the sector operated under a rational, "market-repair" backdrop (real price increases and capital discipline that followed the 2021 consolidation into three nationwide operators), which supported the group's re-rating and share performance. Since around October, our channel checks have pointed to a somewhat more competitive, promotional environment, and the recent results and calls brought that discussion into focus.

Recent months have been characterized by a more competitive entry-level market. The year began broadly in line with our expectations, with operators implementing price adjustments early in the year; unlike last year, however, the pass-through did not extend to prepaid, and promotions linked to the World Cup, leveraging the TV audience, added to the more promotional tone. A notable development was NuCel (Nubank's MVNO on Claro's network), which became more competitive from October 2025, adjusting prices for the second time in the year and expanding to eligible Nubank clients; its reported base has since surpassed one million lines. The operators responded across the low end, with additional ~R$30–45 "Lite/Flex/Fit" hybrid plans, World-Cup-linked promotions, and TIM's new Ultracombo convergent offer (part of it exclusive to São Paulo). The 2Q26 results were consistent with this backdrop: mobile trends decelerated at TIM and Claro, while Vivo held mobile service revenue broadly stable.

The central question the market is trying to answer is whether this proves transitory or more structural. We do not take a firm view at this stage: the key variable is front-book pricing in the control segment, a decision that rests primarily with the postpaid market leaders, and we prefer to see how it evolves before drawing a conclusion. We maintain both names at Market Perform. This report recaps the developments since October, compares the current offers, and summarizes what each operator said on its call.

The four-year market repair

Consolidation restored pricing power. The 2021 exit of Oi Móvel and the split of its assets among Vivo, TIM and Claro left Brazil with the global standard of three disciplined nationwide networks. Since 2020, Vivo's and TIM's mobile service revenue has outpaced inflation and GDP (3-year MSR CAGRs of ~9% and ~8%), supported by that consolidation, the ongoing prepaid-to-control migration, and bundling (Vivo Total). These drivers remain largely in place; the question today is simply whether the recent competitive intensity is a temporary development or a more lasting one.

Lighter capex and discipline turned the sector into a cash-return story, and much of the good news is in the price. Declining capital intensity (FTTH, 5G, the concession-to-authorization migration and network sharing) supported free cash flow and returns, and a global rotation into value and hard assets, with telecom increasingly seen as a near-term AI beneficiary (efficiency gains without the substitution risk affecting some growth sectors), supported the group's re-rating. Both stocks now trade at single-digit 2026 dividend yields (Vivo ~7.5%, TIM ~10%), broadly in line with other bond proxies. With expectations already elevated, recent competitive newsflow has weighed on the shares, and it is that newsflow we turn to next.

What changed since October

Against this backdrop, we group the recent newsflow into three threads: i) NuCel's expansion; ii) the operators' offers; and iii) the compression of entry-level pricing and payment terms. Together, in our view, they help explain the softer channel checks from October onward and the questions around the durability of the repair.

1) NuCel — a fast-growing MVNO

NuCel became more competitive from October 2025. Operated by Nubank on Claro's network under an MVNO model, NuCel revamped its portfolio on October 1 (its second price adjustment of the year), adding data at the same or lower prices and regaining the cheapest-entry position in the market, per the company's own announcement (link). Current plans are 25GB/R$45, 30GB/R$60 and 40GB/R$70, all with unlimited WhatsApp and voice, 5G, no contract, credit-card billing and a "Caixinha" yielding 120% of CDI.

Its main appeal is the absolute entry price and flexibility. The October revamp lowered the price per GB across all tiers while keeping the entry plan at R$45 (see below); combined with no fidelity, fully digital onboarding and a financial-ecosystem benefit (the Caixinha at 120% of CDI), that is what makes the offer competitive at the low end. Its own price history (two adjustments in 2025) illustrates the more competitive stance.

2) Croma and the addressable base

Nubank's new "Croma" package, launched on July 28, includes six months of NuCel at no cost, the most relevant element for telecom. Croma is a new mid-income tier (between the entry-level account and the premium Ultravioleta), with a R$39 monthly fee waived above R$4k/month of spend, and benefits spanning cashback, ChatGPT Go, HBO Max and the free-NuCel offer. The relevant point is distribution: Nubank has over 100 million clients and ~60 million active cards in Brazil, with average spend per card (~R$810/month) still below the market average (~R$1.1k), implying a large pool of under-monetized, mid/upper-income clients.

Even a modest conversion of that base would be relevant for the mobile market. With NuCel already above one million lines before Croma, a bundled six-months-free offer aimed at a large card base is a plausible vector for further share shift at the entry level, the segment where the operators' churn and ARPU are most sensitive. We treat this as an upside risk to NuCel's trajectory and a downside risk to the repair thesis rather than a base case, but it is an important development to monitor.

3) The operators' offers

The entry tier has broadened across all three operators. Vivo introduced "Lite" plans in the ~R$30–45 range (annual/monthly, credit-card billed); Claro has its Flex line; and TIM launched Controle FIT, a credit-card control plan it positions as its most competitive option to close the gap versus peers. These plans increasingly carry streaming, cloud and other digital perks, and the shift toward credit-card billing (with recurring charge) helps reduce bad-debt exposure while supporting recurrence.

Management named the new entry plans on the calls, and there is a discussion in the market on whether they may cannibalize the operators' own higher-value plans. The specific launches cited by management were Vivo Lite (a simpler, credit-card-billed hybrid) and TIM's Controle FIT ("TIM Fit", a credit-card control plan), alongside Claro's Flex line. On both the Vivo and TIM calls, analysts (JP Morgan) asked about the risk that these plans erode the companies' existing higher-priced hybrid/control base. In each case, management framed the plans as incremental and segmented (aimed at prepaid customers who would not otherwise qualify for or migrate to hybrid, with credit-card billing supporting recurrence and limiting bad debt) rather than a repricing of the current base. In our view the cannibalization risk is manageable if the plans mostly capture new, lower-risk customers, and becomes more of a concern if higher-value users trade down, a dynamic we will continue to monitor.

Convergence is the operators' higher-value response, led by TIM's Ultracombo. Launched on July 16, Ultracombo bundles fiber, mobile and content (Globoplay/Paramount+) from ~R$149.99 (standard tier), which TIM positions as up to ~40% cheaper than contracting the services separately (link). Part of the portfolio is exclusive to São Paulo and TIM paired it with a limited-time Father's Day action, illustrating how localized and promotional the go-to-market has become (link). Our channel checks also indicate Claro adjusted combo prices on its site, suggesting the more competitive tone has extended to convergence, not only mobile entry plans.

On a comparable standard combo (~500-600Mbps fiber + ~60-70GB mobile), prices are similar across the three operators. Using each operator's standard entry bundle, monthly prices sit in the ~R$150-160 range, with TIM slightly below Vivo and Claro. The convergence tier has become more promotional, but on standard offers the difference between the three remains relatively contained.

The 2Q26 results reflected this more challenging backdrop. Mobile service revenue growth decelerated at TIM to +4.6% YoY (from +5.6% in 1Q26), converging toward inflation, and at Claro Brasil (AMX) to +6.1% (from +7.8%), the sharpest sequential deceleration among the three, while Vivo remained broadly stable at +6.6% YoY, still comfortably above IPCA. Looking at the trend since 2024, all three operators have decelerated from high-single-digit growth, reinforcing the more challenging dynamics observed through 1H26.

Takeaways from the 2Q26 calls: a common message, different positions

The three calls conveyed a consistent message, even if each operator sits in a different position within it. All three acknowledged a more promotional market, concentrated at the entry level and reinforced by below-the-line offers distributed through new channels; all three have responded with segmented, credit-card-based plans rather than broad repricing; and none signaled an intention to lead prices lower. The differences lie in exposure and positioning: Vivo, growing ahead of peers, described the environment as broadly stable; TIM, whose mobile trends decelerated, provided the most detail on the promotional shift and indicated that front-book adjustments await the larger players; and Claro indicated it is following competitors' moves while noting early signs of a softer economy. That combination (a shared diagnosis, with pricing decisions concentrated at the top of the market) is what frames the transitory-versus-structural question we address in the next section.

Vivo described the environment as broadly stable, while noting pockets of greater competition. On its 2Q26 call, management described the competitive environment as "similar to the previous quarter" (balanced, but more competitive in some markets and segments) and characterized prepaid as the most competitive area, suggesting it "could be more rational." Management detailed Vivo's disciplined, more-for-more approach: front-book price adjustments in March and back-book increases in April (for over 75% of hybrid and ~80% of pure postpaid customers), with postpaid revenue up 7.9% and churn stable at ~1%. It positioned the Lite plans as segmented, low-bad-debt, credit-card products aimed at prepaid customers who would not otherwise migrate to hybrid, rather than a change in strategy.

TIM was the most detailed on the promotional shift and on pricing dynamics. Management attributed the more competitive tone to below-the-line (BTL) offers becoming more widespread after a competitor made one available through an MVNO agreement late last year, prompting others to follow. It acknowledged the mobile base and market share declined on a value-over-volume approach and price-up dilution, and framed Ultracombo, TIM Fit and the PicPay partnership as the portfolio response. On front-book pricing in control, management said TIM "would like to do it" but "we have two competitors with a higher postpaid market share, so let's see what they do." At a subsequent group meeting, management added that it expects front-book increases in 2H26, following last year's sequence in which the leaders moved first (in market-share order: Vivo, then Claro, then TIM).

América Móvil (Claro) described a more promotional market and an incipient macro slowdown. On its call, management said promotions had become more prevalent in both prepaid and postpaid, noting that all three carriers had launched ~R$30 low-end hybrid plans, and that Claro had been following competitors' moves since May ("if they are more aggressive, we need to be a little bit more aggressive"). Management noted tough year-over-year comparisons (2Q25 growth was unusually strong) and said the company was beginning to observe a slowdown in the economy, while still gaining in postpaid portability. Fixed, by contrast, continued to accelerate.

Transitory or structural — what the market is watching

Taken together, the calls point to the question the market is most focused on: whether this movement is transitory or structural. The main variable to watch is front-book pricing in the control segment: continued increases (or at least stable pricing) by the market leaders would point to a cyclical, promotional phase, whereas a sustained move down-market would suggest something more structural. Both our channel checks and TIM's comments indicate the initial move rests with the postpaid market leaders (Vivo and Claro), and last year's adjustments followed that order. A confirmed round of front-book increases in 2H26 would be an important signal on that question.

Vivo (VIVT3)

Against this backdrop, Vivo has been the more resilient of the two names. In 2Q26 it posted solid operating momentum, with mobile service revenue up 6.6% YoY (ahead of peers), postpaid +7.9%, and EBITDA up 10.9% (the first double-digit growth in eleven quarters, with a 41.8% margin). It also maintained pricing discipline through the more competitive period, having adjusted front-book prices in March and raised the back book in April for most of its hybrid and pure-postpaid base. So far, Vivo has managed the more competitive entry-level environment without a visible impact to its premium, convergence-led mix (FTTH +11%, Vivo Total +29%).

For Vivo, the discussion is more about valuation than operations. The cash-return story is solid and well understood (net debt/EBITDA ~0.4x, a commitment to distribute at least 100% of 2026 net income, a BRL 1bn buyback and a ~7.5% dividend yield), but it is largely reflected in the price. The 2Q net-income shortfall versus our estimate was driven by non-operating items (higher D&A from FiBrasil and 5G/FTTH, plus a higher tax rate), with legacy-depreciation relief of ~BRL 0.3bn/quarter expected from 3Q26. Our caution relates less to the quarter itself and more to the risk that a more competitive environment gradually limits the revenue growth embedded in the multiple. Looking ahead, we believe investors are likely to focus on: i) the evolution of the competitive environment; ii) front-book pricing into 2H26; and iii) capital allocation, particularly capex and M&A appetite. We remain on the sidelines, with the stock trading at a ~7.5% 2026 dividend yield.

TIM (TIMS3)

If Vivo has been the more resilient name, TIM is the more exposed in the near term. Its 2Q26 mobile service revenue decelerated to +4.6% YoY (from +5.6% in 1Q26), the mobile base declined, and prepaid fell 6.9%, the profile most sensitive to the low-end/NuCel dynamic. Management noted it would like to raise front-book prices in control but is waiting for the two operators with larger postpaid share to move first (following last year's sequence: Vivo, then Claro, then TIM). Part of TIM's mobile re-acceleration therefore depends on decisions outside its control.

Offsetting that are company-specific initiatives and a better performance elsewhere. EBITDA margin recovered 70bps YoY to 51.5% (after the 1Q shortfall) and net income was above our estimate on lower D&A; fixed grew 27% on V8.Tech and Ultrafibra. Management framed broadband (~2% market share, room for double-digit growth via the I-Systems acquisition and locality-customized convergent offers) and B2B (~6.6% of revenue, reinforced by V8.Tech) as growth avenues, and reiterated capex guidance. Bad debt appears to be plateauing at ~2% (a one-off B2B item, with a possible future provision reversal), and the tax reform is expected to be broadly FCF-neutral. On balance, TIM has more company-specific growth drivers, though its mobile re-acceleration depends in part on the leaders' front-book decisions. Looking ahead, we believe investors are likely to focus on: i) execution on the prepaid-to-control migration and pricing amid the current macro and bad-debt backdrop; ii) the sustainability of the margin recovery; and iii) further details on the convergence strategy following the Ultracombo launch, and its implications for capital allocation. We remain on the sidelines, with the stock trading at a ~10% 2026 dividend yield.

Starlink — a complementary technology

Beyond the competitive dynamics among the operators, a recurring investor question involves Starlink, which we view as complementary to the operators' core rather than a near-term competitor. Starlink's low-earth-orbit service addresses primarily rural and remote areas where terrestrial fixed broadband (FTTH) is less economical, with limited overlap with the operators' urban fixed and mobile bases. Its footprint in underserved areas is becoming more relevant, though: Ookla data reported by Tele.Síntese indicates Starlink is approaching ~10% of fixed-broadband users in rural Brazil (link).

The emerging Direct-to-Device (D2D) model is being structured as a partnership with the operators. In July, Anatel authorized Telefônica Brasil (Vivo) to test D2D (connecting phones directly to satellites without an external antenna) nationwide, and Vivo is reported to be in discussions with SpaceX, potentially becoming the first in Brazil to commercialize it, as reported by Tele.Síntese (link). Anatel's approved D2D framework requires satellite operators to work in partnership with the carriers that hold the relevant spectrum, which supports a complementary, coverage-extension role (filling gaps beyond 5G) rather than a substitutive one, at least initially.

TIM's view is consistent, and frames the risk as longer-dated. At its group meeting, TIM said it has evaluated partnerships with multiple providers, with Starlink the most advanced, but noted that a service comparable to 4G would require the next satellite generation, expected only in 2027. For now, usage is prioritized for dispersed regions and highways, without indoor penetration, and satellite traffic/utilization remains limited. Management sees potential commoditization over the long term as more players compete (which could bring more competitive pricing), and views the more relevant investor concern as longer-dated, namely the prospect of Starlink operating as an MVNO across markets (SpaceX has acquired spectrum in the US); in fixed broadband, satellite could ultimately be complementary to the mobile offering.
