IT Services — a recovery that keeps slipping: downgrading Globant, staying with CI&T

Prices as of [XX-Jun-2026]; figures in USD unless noted. Coverage: Globant (GLOB, Market Perform, YE26 TP US$ [XX]) and CI&T (CINT, Outperform, YE26 TP US$ 7.00). Draft for internal review.

We are downgrading Globant to Market Perform and reiterating our Outperform rating on CI&T. We began 2026 expecting a gradual demand recovery for IT services, with the sector well positioned to capture the derivative of AI implementation inside enterprises. Six months in, that recovery has not materialized: companies have postponed investment decisions amid macro uncertainty, visibility on AI returns remains low, and the latest round of global peer results — Accenture above all — suggests the recovery may be slower than investors were beginning to expect. This backdrop reduces the odds of upside to Globant's already cautious FY26 guidance, at a point when the stock's main driver — growth — is absent; we are moving to the sidelines. CI&T sits on the other side of the same industry: six consecutive quarters of double-digit FXN organic growth and a guidance raise in May keep it as our preferred pick in the segment.

The sector: the recovery keeps slipping

2026 is not playing out as we expected. Our constructive view for the year rested on a simple sequence: enterprises would move AI from pilots to production, and the engineering work required — integration, data, legacy modernization — would land in the backlog of IT services providers. The first half broke that sequence. Adoption is happening, but it is converting into services revenue more slowly than we anticipated, and the industry ended the semester with decelerating bookings, guidance cuts and restructuring programs rather than the long-awaited inflection. In our view, two factors explain the delay.

First, macro: companies are postponing investment decisions. Amid tariffs, higher rates and the geopolitical conflict — a headwind we monitor without overstating — enterprises have deferred precisely the type of large, discretionary technology program that feeds services backlog. The US data illustrates the point: roughly two-thirds of US CFOs said economic uncertainty would lead them to delay or cut capex and hiring, with corporate IT among the most-cited budget lines for cuts (Deloitte CFO Signals, 1Q26). The pattern repeats in this quarter's transcripts across providers: longer decision-making cycles, stricter procurement, and approved projects ramping more slowly.

Second, lack of visibility on returns. The technology is still evolving, and new launches take time to prosper — both of our covered companies have rolled out their own AI-native delivery platforms (detailed in the company sections below), but embedding them in client workflows and proving ROI takes quarters, not weeks; proofs of concept must become production workloads before they move any provider's P&L. Most enterprises cannot yet demonstrate that payback: only 15% of AI decision-makers report an EBITDA lift from AI, and fewer than a third can tie AI's value to P&L changes (Forrester). While the proof doesn't come, budgets wait — Forrester expects large organizations to defer roughly a quarter of planned AI spending into 2027. Beneath the delay sits the more structural fear: AI is deflationary for effort-based delivery. Productivity gains flow quickly into rate cards and discounts, while the offsetting shifts in monetization — outcome- and consumption-based pricing — advance at procurement speed, not at technology speed. Gartner already frames the theme as revenue cannibalization of labor-intensive, high-margin services; IDC projects that 30% of contractual engagements will be outcome-based by 2030. The direction of travel is clear; the transition income statement is not.

The spending mix confirms the diagnosis. Worldwide IT spending should grow 13.5% in 2026, to US$ 6.31 trillion — but the money is flowing around services, not into them. IT services should grow 9.0%, the slowest segment except communication services, while data-center systems grow 55.8% and software 15.1% (Gartner, Apr-26). It is a multi-speed market: hyperscaler infrastructure and AI-centric software capture the AI budget today, and the services layer only monetizes later in the cycle, once implementation scales.

Peer results: the sector's thermometer still lacks visibility

Accenture was the cold shower. We see Accenture as the most relevant thermometer for global IT services demand, with ~4% market share — which is why its fiscal 3Q26 print moved the entire sector. Revenue of US$ 18.7 billion (+6% USD / +3% cc) came in slightly above the midpoint of guidance but below consensus; the real damage was in bookings, at US$ 19.3 billion, down 2% YoY and 13% QoQ — a sharp deceleration from +6% growth in the prior quarter, with consulting growing just 1% cc. More importantly, the company lowered its FY26 revenue growth guidance to 3–4% cc from 3–5%, attributing the revision to four factors: the Middle East conflict (~US$ 100 million of Q3 revenue lost, plus ~US$ 400 million of sales impact in the region), longer decision-making cycles in EMEA, two large managed-services deals deferred to FY27, and the US federal headwind. The stock fell 18%, its worst single-day drop on record; GLOB fell 6% in sympathy and Indian IT names slumped up to 7%. Our take: given that Accenture is perceived as the key proxy for IT services demand, its guidance reset suggests the sector's recovery may be slower than investors were beginning to expect — a negative read-through for the space in general, and for the debate around upside risk to Globant's guidance in particular.
