Telecom Brazil — Is the repair cracking? Tracking a tougher competitive cycle

Vivo (VIVT3) — Market Perform, TP BRL 38 | TIM (TIMS3) — Market Perform, TP BRL 30. Prices/estimates as of [XX-Mon-2026]; BRL unless noted. Draft for internal review.

Overview — what changed, and why the stocks fell

Vivo and TIM sold off this week after their 2Q26 results, and we have been fielding the same question from investors: what actually changed? For roughly four years, the sector operated under a rational, "market-repair" backdrop — real price increases and capital discipline that followed the 2021 consolidation into three nationwide operators — and that regime was the engine of the group's re-rating and strong share performance. Since around October, however, our channel checks have flagged a more competitive, promotional environment, and the recent results and calls brought that debate to the surface.

The story of the last few months is, at its core, a tougher entry-level market. The catalyst was NuCel — Nubank's MVNO on Claro's network — which turned materially more aggressive from October 2025, cutting prices for the second time in the year and expanding to eligible Nubank clients; its reported base has since crossed one million lines. The incumbents responded across the low end, with a wave of ~R$30–45 "Lite/Flex/Fit" hybrid plans, World-Cup-linked promotions, and TIM's new Ultracombo convergent offer (part of it exclusive to São Paulo). This week's results were consistent with the shift: mobile trends decelerated at TIM and Claro, while Vivo held mobile service revenue broadly stable.

The key debate is whether this is a temporary detour or a structural break. Our own read, corroborated by management commentary on the calls, is that the repair is being tested rather than broken — the swing factor is front-book pricing in the control segment, and that decision rests primarily with the postpaid market leaders. We keep both names at Market Perform. This report recaps the developments since October, compares the current offers, distils what each operator said on its call, and lays out a framework to monitor the competition going forward (our Competitive Index).

The four-year repair — the baseline now under test

Consolidation is what restored pricing power. The 2021 exit of Oi Móvel and the split of its assets among Vivo, TIM and Claro left Brazil with the global standard of three disciplined nationwide networks. Since 2020, Vivo's and TIM's mobile service revenue has outpaced inflation and GDP (3-year MSR CAGRs of ~9% and ~8%), supported by that consolidation, the steady prepaid-to-control migration, and bundling (Vivo Total). These drivers remain largely in place; the question today is whether the recent competitive intensity marks a genuine break from this backdrop or a temporary detour.

Lighter capex and discipline turned the sector into a cash-return story — and the good news was in the price. Declining capital intensity (FTTH, 5G, the concession-to-authorization migration and network sharing) lifted free cash flow and returns, and a global rotation into value and hard assets — with telecom increasingly seen as a near-term AI winner (efficiency gains without the substitution risk hitting some growth sectors) — re-rated the group. Both stocks now trade at single-digit 2026 dividend yields (Vivo ~7.5%, TIM ~10%), broadly in line with other bond proxies. With expectations elevated, the bar for disappointment was low — which is why the competitive newsflow has hit the shares.

What changed since October — the competitive turn

We group the recent newsflow into three threads: NuCel's escalation, the incumbents' offers, and the compression of entry-level pricing and payment terms. Together they explain why the checks deteriorated from October onward and why investors are questioning the durability of the repair.

1) NuCel — from a rounding error to a one-million-line disruptor

NuCel turned decisively more aggressive from October 2025. Operated by Nubank on Claro's network under an MVNO model, NuCel revamped its portfolio on October 1 — its second price cut of the year — adding data at the same or lower prices and regaining the cheapest-entry position in the market (link). Sell-side coverage flagged the move as an explicit competitive response (link). Current plans are 25GB/R$45, 30GB/R$60 and 40GB/R$70 — all with unlimited WhatsApp and voice, 5G, no contract, credit-card billing and a "Caixinha" yielding 120% of CDI.

Its edge is the absolute entry ticket and flexibility. The October revamp lowered the price per GB across all tiers while keeping the entry plan at R$45 (see below); combined with no fidelity, fully digital onboarding and a financial-ecosystem benefit (the Caixinha at 120% of CDI), that is what makes the offer disruptive at the low end. Its own price history — two cuts in 2025 — is the clearest evidence of the escalation.

The reported base masks the true ramp — and it has now inflected sharply. Per Anatel, NuCel's reported base sat frozen at ~60k from mid-2025 through December (a disclosure gap), before being reported at 660k in January and above one million lines by mid-2026 (link). Whether one reads the January jump as a genuine acceleration or a catch-up of the frozen months, the trajectory is unambiguous: NuCel went from immaterial to a seven-figure base in about a year, concentrated in the most recent months.

2) Croma and the addressable base — why the ceiling could be higher

Nubank's new "Croma" package, launched on July 28, bundles six months of free NuCel — and that is the part that matters for telecom. Croma is a new mid-income tier (between the entry-level account and the premium Ultravioleta), with a R$39 monthly fee waived above R$4k/month of spend, and benefits spanning cashback, ChatGPT Go, HBO Max and the free-NuCel offer. The strategic point is distribution: Nubank has over 100 million clients and ~60 million active cards in Brazil, with average spend per card (~R$810/month) still below the market average (~R$1.1k), implying a large pool of under-monetized, mid/upper-income clients.

Even a modest conversion of that base would be material for the mobile market. With NuCel already past one million lines before Croma, a bundled six-months-free hook aimed at tens of millions of engaged card users is a credible vector for further share shift at the entry level — precisely the segment where the incumbents' churn and ARPU are most exposed. We treat this as an upside risk to NuCel's trajectory and a downside risk to the repair thesis, rather than a base case, but it is the single most important structural development to monitor.

3) The incumbents' offers — down-market breadth and convergence

The entry tier has broadened across all three operators. Vivo rolled out "Lite" plans in the ~R$30–45 range (annual/monthly, credit-card billed); Claro has its Flex line; and TIM launched Controle FIT, a credit-card control plan it positions as its most competitive option to close the gap versus peers. These plans increasingly carry streaming, cloud and other digital perks, and the shift toward credit-card billing (with recurring charge) lowers bad-debt exposure while locking in recurrence.

Management named the new entry plans on the calls — and a live debate is whether they cannibalize the operators' own higher-value plans. The specific launches cited by management were Vivo Lite (a simpler, credit-card-billed hybrid) and TIM's Controle FIT ("TIM Fit", a credit-card control plan), alongside Claro's Flex line. On both the Vivo and TIM calls, analysts (JP Morgan) asked directly about the risk that these plans erode the companies' existing higher-priced hybrid/control base. In each case, management framed the plans as incremental and segmented — aimed at prepaid customers who would not otherwise qualify for or migrate to hybrid, with credit-card billing securing recurrence and limiting bad debt — rather than a repricing of the current base. In our view the cannibalization risk is real but manageable if the plans genuinely capture new, lower-risk customers, and becomes a concern only if higher-value users trade down — a dynamic our Competitive Index is designed to track.

Convergence is the incumbents' higher-value answer, led by TIM's Ultracombo. Launched on July 16, Ultracombo bundles fiber, mobile and content (Globoplay/Paramount+) from R$89.99, which TIM positions as up to ~40% cheaper than buying the services separately (link). Notably, part of the portfolio is exclusive to São Paulo and TIM paired it with a limited-time Father's Day action, underscoring how localized and promotional the go-to-market has become (link). Our channel checks (and forwarded messages from the field) also indicate Claro cut combo prices on its site in response — a sign the promotional tone is spreading into convergence, not just mobile entry plans.

What management said on the calls

Vivo framed the environment as broadly stable but acknowledged pockets of aggression. On its 2Q26 call, CEO Christian Gebara described the competitive environment as "similar to the previous quarter" — balanced, but more aggressive in some markets and segments — and characterized prepaid as the most competitive area, which he argued "could be more rational." He detailed Vivo's disciplined, more-for-more approach: front-book price adjustments in March and back-book increases in April (for over 75% of hybrid and ~80% of pure postpaid customers), with postpaid revenue up 7.9% and churn steady at ~1%. He positioned the Lite plans as segmented, low-bad-debt, credit-card products aimed at prepaid customers who would not otherwise migrate to hybrid, rather than a change in strategy.

TIM was the most explicit about the promotional shift and the pricing standoff. CEO Alberto Griselli attributed the tougher tone to below-the-line (BTL) offers becoming more widespread after a competitor made one available through an MVNO agreement late last year, prompting others to follow. He acknowledged the mobile base and market share declined on a value-over-volume stance and price-up dilution, and framed Ultracombo, TIM Fit and the PicPay partnership as the portfolio response. Crucially, on front-book pricing in control, he said TIM "would like to do it" but "we have two competitors with a higher postpaid market share, so let's see what they do." At a subsequent group meeting, management added that it expects front-book increases in 2H26, following last year's sequence in which the leaders moved first (in market-share order: Vivo, then Claro, then TIM).

América Móvil (Claro) confirmed a more promotional market and an incipient macro slowdown. On its call, CEO Daniel Hajj said promotions had become more prevalent in both prepaid and postpaid, noting that all three carriers had launched ~R$30 low-end hybrid plans, and that Claro had been following competitors' aggressiveness since May ("if they are more aggressive, we need to be a little bit more aggressive"). He flagged tough year-over-year comparisons (2Q25 growth was unusually strong) and said the company was beginning to feel a slowdown in the economy, while still gaining in postpaid portability. Fixed, by contrast, continued to accelerate.

How do we get comfort that this is transitory?

The decisive signal is front-book pricing in the control segment. The repair holds if the market leaders continue to raise — or at least hold — front-book prices in control/postpaid; it is more at risk if they chase volume down-market. Both our channel checks and TIM's own comments point to the same conclusion: the initial move sits with the postpaid market leaders (Vivo and Claro), and last year's adjustments followed that order. A confirmed round of front-book increases in 2H26 would be the clearest evidence that the promotional intensity is cyclical rather than structural.

Vivo (VIVT3)

Vivo is the more resilient of the two names going into the competitive turn. In 2Q26 it posted the strongest operating momentum in the sector — mobile service revenue up 6.6% YoY (ahead of peers), postpaid +7.9%, and EBITDA up 10.9% (its first double-digit print in eleven quarters, margin 41.8%). It also kept pricing discipline through the promotional noise, having adjusted front-book prices in March and raised the back book in April for most of its hybrid and pure-postpaid base. So far, in other words, Vivo has absorbed the tougher entry-level competition without visible damage to its premium, convergence-led mix (FTTH +11%, Vivo Total +29%).

The debate on Vivo is valuation, not operations. The cash-return story is strong and well understood — net debt/EBITDA ~0.4x, a commitment to distribute at least 100% of 2026 net income, a BRL 1bn buyback and a ~7.5% dividend yield — but it is largely in the price. The 2Q net-income miss was driven by non-operating items (higher D&A from FiBrasil and 5G/FTTH, plus a higher tax rate), with legacy-depreciation relief of ~BRL 0.3bn/quarter coming from 3Q26. The rationale for our caution is therefore not the quarter itself but the risk that the intensifying competition eventually caps the revenue growth the multiple is counting on.

TIM (TIMS3)

TIM is the more exposed of the two to the competitive turn in the near term. Its 2Q26 mobile service revenue decelerated to +4.6% YoY (from +5.6% in 1Q26), the mobile base declined, and prepaid fell 6.9% — the profile most sensitive to the low-end/NuCel dynamic. Tellingly, management said it would like to raise front-book prices in control but is waiting for the two operators with larger postpaid share to move first (following last year's sequence: Vivo, then Claro, then TIM). In other words, part of TIM's mobile re-acceleration is not in its own hands — the clearest link in our coverage between the sector debate and a single name.

Offsetting that are genuine self-help levers and a better quarter elsewhere. EBITDA margin recovered 70bps YoY to 51.5% (after the 1Q miss) and net income beat on lower D&A; fixed grew 27% on V8.Tech and Ultrafibra. Management framed broadband (~2% market share, room for double-digit growth via the I-Systems acquisition and locality-customized convergent offers) and B2B (~6.6% of revenue, reinforced by V8.Tech) as growth avenues, and reiterated capex guidance. Bad debt appears to be plateauing at ~2% (a one-off B2B item, with a possible future provision reversal), and the tax reform is expected to be broadly FCF-neutral. Net-net, TIM offers more self-help and a higher (~10%) dividend yield, but the fix for mobile is partly gated by the leaders' front-book decisions.

Starlink — complementary today, a longer-dated watch item

We see satellite as complementary to the operators' core rather than a near-term competitor. Starlink's low-earth-orbit service addresses primarily rural and remote areas where terrestrial fixed broadband (FTTH) is uneconomic — limited overlap with the incumbents' urban fixed and mobile bases. Its footprint in underserved areas is becoming more relevant, though: recent press, citing Ookla, indicates Starlink is approaching ~10% of fixed-broadband users in rural Brazil (link).

Importantly, the emerging Direct-to-Device (D2D) model is being built as a partnership with the operators, not around them. In July, Anatel authorized Telefônica Brasil (Vivo) to test D2D — connecting phones directly to satellites without an external antenna — nationwide, and Vivo is reported to be negotiating with SpaceX, potentially becoming the first in Brazil to commercialize it (link) (link). Crucially, Anatel's approved D2D framework requires satellite operators to work in partnership with the carriers that hold the relevant spectrum — reinforcing the complementary, coverage-extension role (filling gaps beyond 5G) rather than a substitutive one, at least initially.

TIM's read is consistent, and frames the risk as longer-dated. At its group meeting, TIM said it has evaluated partnerships with multiple providers, with Starlink the most advanced, but noted that a service comparable to 4G would require the next satellite generation, expected only in 2027. For now, usage is prioritized for dispersed regions and highways, without indoor penetration, and satellite traffic/utilization remains limited. Management sees potential commoditization over the long term as large players compete (bringing more competitive pricing), and believes the genuine investor concern is longer-dated — the prospect of Starlink operating as an MVNO across markets (SpaceX has acquired spectrum in the US) — while in fixed broadband satellite could ultimately be complementary to the mobile offering.
