Global software beta and the Brazilian names: reading IGV as a cohort barometer

Constituent weights as of [XX-Mon-2026]; ETF figures in USD.

Listed software trades as a cohort. Across geographies the group shares a single factor profile — long-duration, high-multiple, rate-sensitive growth — and tends to re-rate together when the market's appetite for software risk shifts, largely independent of company-specific fundamentals. The Brazilian software names sit inside this dynamic: TOTVS, LWSA and VTEX are comparatively small and less liquid, and their price action is increasingly tethered to the global software complex rather than to local newsflow alone. The most liquid, transparent and tradable expression of that complex is the iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF (IGV). With a deep, actively traded options market, it is the instrument of choice for taking or hedging software-factor exposure, which makes it both a mirror and a transmission channel for sentiment in the group. That dual role is why IGV has become the reference barometer investors watch to gauge the cohort's beta — and, by extension, the backdrop against which the Brazilian names move.

Its relevance has risen with the AI cycle. The latest generation of models has injected a structural debate into the software thesis: whether generative AI compresses switching costs — lowering the cost to migrate off, or rebuild, incumbent systems — and whether it erodes seat-based monetization as agents begin to perform work previously billed per user. The debate cuts both ways, since incumbents with proprietary data, distribution and deeply embedded workflows may instead deepen lock-in and open new consumption- or outcome-based revenue. What matters for price action is that this narrative re-rates the entire software factor in waves, and it does not stop at the border: each rotation between "AI as disruption" and "AI as tailwind" feeds directly into Brazilian software multiples. TOTVS — a management-software incumbent whose moat rests on switching costs and largely recurring, per-user economics — sits squarely in that debate, as do LWSA and VTEX in their niches. Watching IGV is therefore, in part, watching how the market prices an AI-on-software narrative that simultaneously prices the local names.

What IGV is, precisely. It is an ETF, not an index — it passively tracks the S&P North American Expanded Technology Software Index. The eligibility universe is restricted to the US and Canada, covering software companies plus selected names from interactive home entertainment and interactive media. It holds roughly 110 positions, is modified market-cap weighted with capping rules to limit single-name concentration, rebalances quarterly and reconstitutes semi-annually, and carries an expense ratio of 0.39%. The fund is non-diversified and highly concentrated: in the snapshot analyzed here, the top ten names accounted for roughly 60% of the fund, led by Palo Alto Networks (9.2%), Microsoft (8.4%), Palantir (8.0%), Oracle (7.3%), CrowdStrike (6.7%), Salesforce (4.9%), AppLovin (4.5%), Cadence (4.1%), ServiceNow (3.9%) and Fortinet (3.6%). The geographic rule matters for the Brazilian comparison: it excludes SAP and Sage, arguably the closest structural peers to TOTVS in ERP and SMB management software.

This linkage has been tightening. The co-movement between TOTVS and IGV has risen over time, as global software sentiment — and, more recently, the AI re-rating — has come to weigh on the local tape more than idiosyncratic, company-specific newsflow. That is what motivates this piece. Rather than take the read-across at face value, the aim here is to dissect IGV into its pockets — the distinct business types that coexist inside a single "software" label — to understand what each one is and what drives its performance. Mapping those pockets is the prerequisite for the question that follows: whether it makes sense to lean increasingly on IGV to explain TOTVS's price action, or whether only a minority of the index genuinely shares TOTVS's drivers while the rest moves on agendas of its own. As a first step, we classified all ~108 holdings into eight macro buckets and scored each on adherence to TOTVS's profile through four filters.

The filters are anchored in what TOTVS does, framed qualitatively. The core is ERP/management software; the RD Station franchise adds a front-office layer of CRM and marketing automation; and Techfin embeds credit and payments into the ERP. The franchise is built around SMB and mid-market clients and a recurring, cloud-delivered model. The four filters follow: (i) function — position in that stack, from ERP and vertical management through CRM/front-office and embedded fintech; (ii) customer — SMB/mid-market is TOTVS's signature, so enterprise-only, financial-institution, government and consumer names score lower; (iii) model — recurring SaaS/cloud plus embedded payments versus license, hardware, advertising, royalties or crypto exposure; and (iv) secular theme — digitization of business management versus the chip cycle, crypto prices, the ad cycle or the threat landscape.

The result argues for caution on the fundamental read. Only about 28% of IGV (high plus medium adherence) maps onto TOTVS's business at all, and roughly 72% is weakly or unrelated (low plus no adherence). The single largest exposure outside the management theme is cybersecurity at ~21% of the fund — a block nearly as large as the entire ERP/management-and-fintech bucket (~22%), and almost all of it low-adherence. Cyber, semiconductor design (Cadence and Synopsys, ~7.5% combined), diversified mega-cap (Microsoft, 8.4%), advertising/consumer names, crypto miners (~4%) and gaming (~4%) sit at the top of the weighting and have little to do with how an ERP franchise earns its revenue.

The concentration cuts the other way for the names that do fit. The management-and-fintech bucket is the largest in the index, but its weight is dominated by a handful of large, enterprise-leaning names — Oracle (7.3%), Salesforce (4.9%), Intuit (2.8%) and Workday (0.9%) alone are ~85% of the entire high-adherence weight. The constituents that most faithfully replicate TOTVS's model — SMB management software with embedded fintech — are scattered across the tail in fractions of a percent: ServiceTitan (0.15%), AppFolio (0.14%), Lightspeed (0.04%), Agilysys (0.08%), Vertex (0.03%) and Blackbaud (0.03%). HubSpot, the cleanest analog to RD Station, is 0.35%. The closest comparables exist in IGV but carry almost no weight, while the heaviest names are the least comparable.

This is where the balanced read lands. IGV is a strong instrument through one lens and a weak one through another. As a factor and sentiment gauge — software risk appetite, duration and multiple dynamics, positioning, and, increasingly, how the market is discounting the AI-on-software narrative — it is genuinely informative for the Brazilian cohort, and the tightening correlation is real on those terms; that is precisely why the link "works" in the short run. As a fundamental proxy, it is poor: roughly three-quarters of the index is driven by demand cycles (threats, chips, crypto, advertising) that have no bearing on TOTVS's drivers, and the pockets that do share those drivers are immaterial to the fund's behavior. The practical implication is not to discard IGV but to use it for what it measures — sentiment, the multiple regime and the AI re-rating — while reading the pocket-level performance underneath the headline, and to pair it, for any fundamental read-across, with a curated comp set of SMB-focused management software and embedded-fintech names (HubSpot, Intuit, ServiceTitan, Lightspeed, BILL) plus the global ERP peers the index leaves out by geography (SAP, Sage).

Improving the read-across

To make the index a cleaner read-across for the local cohort, the natural step is to strip out the names with no business adherence at all and re-weight the remainder — leaving the low-adherence software names (cybersecurity, infrastructure, dev tools) in place, since at least they share the factor profile. Twenty-eight constituents, some 30.8% of the fund, fall in the "no adherence" bucket, and they cluster into a few clearly off-theme groups: a diversified mega-cap whose ERP exposure is immaterial (Microsoft, 8.4%); consumer advertising technology (AppLovin, 4.5%); semiconductor-design software tied to the chip cycle (Cadence and Synopsys, ~7.6% combined); video-game publishers (EA, Take-Two, Unity, ~3.8%); crypto miners and digital-asset treasuries that are effectively levered plays on Bitcoin and Ethereum (Strategy, MARA, Riot, Hut 8 and peers, ~4.2%); IP-licensing royalty businesses (InterDigital, Adeia, Dolby); and consumer/deep-tech names (Snap, Life360, Gen Digital, D-Wave, Aurora). None of these is a genuine TOTVS comparable, and removing them deletes no high- or medium-adherence holding. Renormalizing the remaining 69.2% of the fund to 100% lifts the share of business-aligned weight (high plus medium adherence) from roughly 28% to about 41%, with high-adherence names alone rising from ~19% to ~27%. The trade-off is transparency about what remains: low-adherence software — overwhelmingly cybersecurity and infrastructure — becomes the majority of the slimmed index at ~59%, the honest cost of keeping the factor-aligned names rather than purging them too. The adjusted basket is a better fundamental mirror of an ERP/SMB-management business, but it remains a software-beta construct rather than a pure ERP comp set; for the latter, a curated peer list (HubSpot, Intuit, ServiceTitan, Lightspeed, BILL, plus the geographically excluded SAP and Sage) is still the sharper instrument.
