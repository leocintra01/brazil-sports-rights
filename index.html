IT Services — a recovery that keeps slipping: downgrading Globant, staying with CI&T

Prices as of [XX-Jun-2026]; figures in USD unless noted. Coverage: Globant (GLOB, Market Perform, YE26 TP US$ [XX]) and CI&T (CINT, Outperform, YE26 TP US$ 7.00). Draft for internal review.

We are downgrading Globant to Market Perform and reiterating our Outperform rating on CI&T. We began 2026 expecting a gradual demand recovery for IT services, with the sector well positioned to capture the derivative of AI implementation inside enterprises. Six months in, that recovery has not materialized: companies have postponed investment decisions amid macro uncertainty, visibility on AI returns remains low, and the latest round of global peer results — Accenture above all — suggests the recovery may be slower than investors were beginning to expect. This backdrop reduces the odds of upside to Globant's already cautious FY26 guidance, at a point when the stock's main driver — growth — is absent; we are moving to the sidelines. CI&T sits on the other side of the same industry: six consecutive quarters of double-digit FXN organic growth and a guidance raise in May keep it as our preferred pick in the segment.

The sector: the recovery keeps slipping

2026 is not playing out as we expected. Our constructive view for the year rested on a simple sequence: enterprises would move AI from pilots to production, and the engineering work required — integration, data, legacy modernization — would land in the backlog of IT services providers. The first half broke that sequence. Adoption is happening, but it is converting into services revenue more slowly than we anticipated, and the industry ended the semester with decelerating bookings, guidance cuts and restructuring programs rather than the long-awaited inflection. In our view, two factors explain the delay.

First, macro: companies are postponing investment decisions. Amid tariffs, higher rates and the geopolitical conflict — a headwind we monitor without overstating — enterprises have deferred precisely the type of large, discretionary technology program that feeds services backlog. The US data illustrates the point: roughly two-thirds of US CFOs said economic uncertainty would lead them to delay or cut capex and hiring, with corporate IT among the most-cited budget lines for cuts (Deloitte CFO Signals, 1Q26). The pattern repeats in this quarter's transcripts across providers: longer decision-making cycles, stricter procurement, and approved projects ramping more slowly.

Second, lack of visibility on returns. The technology is still evolving and new launches take time to prosper — both of our covered companies have rolled out AI-native delivery platforms (detailed below), but embedding them in client workflows and proving ROI takes quarters, not weeks. Buyers want proof before scaling, and most cannot show it yet: per Forrester, only 15% of AI decision-makers report an EBITDA lift from AI, and fewer than a third can tie its value to P&L changes. While the proof doesn't come, budgets wait — Forrester expects enterprises to defer a quarter of planned AI spend into 2027, and Gartner predicts that over 40% of agentic-AI projects will be canceled by end-2027, "due to escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls." Beneath the delay sits the deflation fear. AI is deflationary for effort-based delivery: productivity gains flow quickly into rate cards and discounts, while the offsetting monetization shifts — outcome- and consumption-based pricing — move at procurement speed, not technology speed. Forrester describes the supply side of the same mechanism: as demand slips, "providers will chase fill rate with discounts and oversized commitments." Gartner already frames the theme as revenue cannibalization of labor-intensive, high-margin services, and IDC projects that, driven by agentic AI, "30% of all contractual engagements with service providers will be outcome-based" by 2029. The direction of travel is clear; the transition income statement is not.

The spending mix confirms the diagnosis. Worldwide IT spending should grow 13.5% in 2026, to US$ 6.31 trillion — but the money is flowing around services, not into them. As the exhibit below shows, IT services should grow just 9.0% — the slowest segment except communication services — while data-center systems grow 55.8% and software 15.1% (Gartner, Apr-26). It is a multi-speed market: hyperscaler infrastructure and AI-centric software capture the AI budget today, and the services layer only monetizes later in the cycle, once implementation scales.

Peer results: the sector's thermometer still lacks visibility

Fragmented sector, but a readable sentiment. IT services is a fragmented industry, and client books are heterogeneous — exposure varies by geography, vertical, contract type and the split between discretionary projects and managed services, which is why results rarely move in lockstep. Even so, each earnings season works as a thermometer: read together, the prints reveal a sentiment that no single company can. This season, the reading was unambiguous — and more cautious into 2027. The same themes repeat across transcripts regardless of business model: decelerating bookings and book-to-bill at or below 1x, deals deferred rather than cancelled, discretionary work cut first, guidance reset lower, restructuring programs to defend margins, and buybacks doing the work that growth used to do. We run through the most relevant prints below.

Accenture set the tone for the season. The most relevant thermometer for global IT services demand (~4% market share) posted fiscal 3Q26 revenue of US$ 18.7 billion (+6% USD / +3% cc), slightly below consensus — but the damage was in bookings: US$ 19.3 billion, down 2% YoY and 13% QoQ, after growing 6% in the prior quarter. More importantly, the company cut FY26 revenue growth guidance to 3–4% cc (from 3–5%), citing the Middle East conflict (~US$ 100 million of revenue lost in the quarter, plus ~US$ 400 million of regional sales impact), longer decision-making cycles in EMEA, two large managed-services deals deferred to FY27 and the US federal headwind. The stock fell 18% — its worst single-day drop on record — with GLOB down 6% in sympathy. Our take: when the key proxy for IT services demand resets guidance on soft bookings and deferred contracts, the recovery is slower than investors were beginning to expect — a negative read-through for the space in general, and for the upside debate around Globant's guidance in particular. Management also noted that AI infrastructure spending has not yet translated into services demand — the same sequencing that anchors our sector view.

Endava shows what happens to discretionary digital work in this tape. Among listed peers, Endava is the closest business-model comparison to the LatAm digital-engineering names — mid-sized, project-based, discretionary — and its fiscal 3Q26 was the most dramatic print of the season: revenue fell 8.4% YoY (−6.4% cc), the adjusted PBT margin collapsed to 1.8% from 12.6%, the company recognized a £364.6 million goodwill impairment, and FY26 guidance was reset to a 5.0–6.0% cc decline. Management described the period as one of the most challenging in years, with clients scrutinizing technology spending more carefully than at any point since the macro slowdown began. The message for the space is uncomfortable: when budgets tighten, the project-based digital book — the same profile of work that drove the sector's 2020–22 boom — is the first line cut.

DXC confirms that the pressure extends into FY27. The legacy-heavy end of the industry is contracting outright: fiscal 4Q organic revenue fell 6.6% YoY, bookings dropped 13.5%, and the full-year book-to-bill of 0.98x left the company guiding FY27 revenue to US$ 12.11–12.35 billion — another annual decline. The stock fell 21.5% on the release. DXC's problems are partly self-inflicted, but a book-to-bill below 1x at a US$ 12+ billion revenue base tells us the replacement demand for large managed-services contracts is simply not showing up — consistent with the deferrals Accenture reported.

Wipro put an explicit contraction on the table for FY27. The company guided its 1Q FY27 to −2.0% to 0% cc — an outright sequential decline to open the fiscal year — with 4Q bookings down 13.9% YoY and large-deal TCV down 18.5%. Management attributed the caution to macro uncertainty, client indecision and slower deal cycles, with financial services clients particularly conservative outside India. The Rs 15,000 crore buyback announced alongside works as a valuation floor, not as a growth answer — a capital-allocation playbook we increasingly see across the sector, Globant included.

Cognizant is restructuring its way through the transition. The 1Q26 numbers beat estimates, but the more telling disclosure was Project Leap: US$ 230–320 million in charges, mostly severance, targeting US$ 200–300 million in annual savings. In our reading, this is the industry acknowledging that AI-enabled delivery requires structurally fewer people — margins are being defended by cost reduction, not supported by growth. Despite the operational beat, the stock trades near 52-week lows, down 33% YTD: the market is not paying for cost programs in a growth-starved sector.

Infosys anchored expectations for a weak 2027. The quarter itself beat (revenue of US$ 5.04 billion vs. US$ 4.98 billion expected), but FY27 guidance of +1.5% to +3.5% cc came in below the ~2–5% the market anticipated, and large-deal TCV decelerated to US$ 3.2 billion from US$ 4.8 billion in the prior quarter. Management pointed to slower decision-making and clients reassessing tech budgets in light of AI productivity opportunities — deflation showing up as a demand argument. For us, this was the clearest early signal that the recovery debate had shifted from "2H26" to "some point in 2027."

The common thread: this is an industry-wide condition, not an idiosyncratic one. Across six of the most relevant global prints of the season, the pattern repeats — bookings decelerating, guidance cut or reset lower, restructuring programs multiplying, and buybacks doing the work that growth used to do. This matters for how we read Globant: the deterioration is a sector condition, which supports our view that patience will be required across the space. But it also raises the bar for any individual name to outgrow it — which makes CI&T's numbers, below, all the more notable.

Our view: a fragmented market that still rewards differentiation — but a longer wait. IT services remains a fragmented market, with performance varying by client base, vertical exposure and geography, and we continue to believe that providers with genuine know-how, proprietary delivery platforms and the ability to optimize token economics are well positioned for the implementation cycle, which is still very early stage. Lower unit costs should ultimately expand the number of viable AI use cases rather than shrink the workload. IDC frames the split in similar terms — for the IT industry, agent adoption is "both a windfall and a reckoning": providers that enable this scale efficiently "will dominate the market," while those tied to legacy delivery models "will see margins shrink as automation commoditizes legacy value propositions." But we recognize the recovery is taking longer than we expected, and we prefer to anchor 2027 on the cautious base peers have already set — Infosys at +1.5–3.5% cc, Wipro opening the year in contraction, DXC declining — rather than underwrite a reacceleration we cannot yet see in bookings. In the meantime, the sector's de-rating has been indiscriminate, while operational performance has not: the momentum decoupling between CI&T and Globant is the widest in our coverage history, and it drives the rating actions below.

Globant: downgrading to Market Perform

Why we are stepping to the sidelines. For the past several months, our Outperform case on Globant rested on a simple construction: consensus estimates were already bearish, the stock was trading at historical-low multiples, and the thesis needed only one ingredient to work — the first visible sign of a growth inflection, which we expected the 2Q26 guidance to deliver. That sign did not come. The guidance pointed to stabilization rather than recovery, the sector backdrop deteriorated after Accenture's reset, and the company's exposures — the Middle East and travel-related verticals — sit precisely where demand visibility is weakest. In this context, patience is no longer enough: with the main driver investors like in this case still absent, we are downgrading the stock to Market Perform.

The trigger we were waiting for came — and it came flat. Since our March update, we had flagged the 2Q26 guidance as the main trigger investors were waiting for and a sensitive KPI for us to reassess the thesis, as it should indicate a modest sequential rebound in growth. Instead, the company guided the quarter roughly flat YoY at the midpoint. The 1Q26 print itself was competent — revenue above the high end of guidance — but it marked the second consecutive quarterly decline, was flattered by FX, and came with a client base that keeps narrowing at the margin: accounts above US$ 1 million fell again, to 333 from 341 a year ago. For a franchise that compounded at 37% in 2022 and 18% in 2023, a full-year outlook of roughly +1% is not a base from which re-ratings are built. Therefore, however cheap the stock looks, we struggle to identify what would move it higher in the near term.

The debate around upside risk to the guidance has become more challenging. After Accenture's cut, we see a lower probability of a positive revision to Globant's initially cautious outlook — and the construction of the second half reinforces that caution. Management attributes the expected acceleration to four specific engagements ramping through the year, helped by a higher number of billable days: a concentrated, execution-dependent bridge, in our view, for a market where discretionary demand keeps softening. Meanwhile, the book remains weighted toward effort-priced work — the kind clients defer first — and North America, more than half of revenues, is still declining. As a result, we see the risks around the second half skewed to the downside, not the upside.

The Middle East went from growth engine to swing factor. The region — roughly two-thirds of New Markets, or ~4% of total revenues — had been one of the few sources of incremental growth in the story. It is now the variable that defines the shape of the guidance: management acknowledged that the midpoint assumes the region remains broadly stable, that the low end contemplates a meaningful deterioration, and that the conflict was the reason for not raising the outlook despite the first-quarter beat — a prudent stance, in our view, but one that captures how fragile visibility has become. The precedent argues for respect: the same disruption cost Accenture ~US$ 100 million in quarterly revenue plus ~US$ 400 million in regional sales, and New Markets revenue already fell 7% YoY in 1Q26.

Travel adds a second layer to the same debate. Travel- and hospitality-related accounts represent ~13% of revenues, and management flagged emerging concerns around airline clients as fuel prices rise. The mitigation offered captures the sector's dilemma well: the company's two largest airline accounts are migrating to AI Pods in multi-year arrangements — supportive for margins, but deflationary for revenue per unit of work. That said, the largest media account was described as recovering versus 2025, which softens part of the concern. The exhibits below summarize both exposures.

On AI Pods, the narrative is advancing faster than the P&L. This is the part of the story working best — and it deserves credit. ARR is compounding quickly from a small base, the pipeline keeps expanding, the model is already present in 40% of the top-20 accounts with a stated path toward 70%, and not a single client has moved back to the traditional model. The economics are also attractive, with margins running materially above the consolidated level. The problem is scale: management itself concedes that this year's numbers embed very little contribution from the model, which still represents around 1% of revenue. At this point, we prefer to treat AI Pods as the option that could eventually rebuild the growth story — not as an offset to the pressure on the other 99% of the book.

The Anthropic alliance strengthens that option, not this year's numbers. In late June, Globant announced a multi-year alliance with Anthropic, introducing Claude-powered AI Pods and becoming one of the first Preferred Services Partners in the Claude Partner Network — the largest LatAm-born services company to reach that status. Beyond extending Claude access to the entire workforce and adding engineering-certification programs, the alliance targets precisely the verticals where Globant is most exposed: media & entertainment, gaming, airlines and hospitality. It also speaks to a debate that dominated the earnings call — model providers moving into the services layer — which management frames as both competition and partnership. We read the deal as a positive signal for the AI Pods pipeline; the timeline of its impact, however, belongs to 2027, not 2026.

While growth is absent, management is defending profitability — and the floor. Margins held within the guided range despite FX headwinds and wage pressure in key delivery hubs; headcount keeps falling while revenue per employee rises; and capital allocation has turned decisively toward the stock itself, with a new buyback that, combined with the previous program, approaches 15% of the market cap — funded by the first positive first-quarter FCF since 2019 and a projected ~13% FCF yield for the year. Management was candid that it views its own equity as the most compelling use of capital at current multiples. We agree on the arithmetic; the implication, however, cuts both ways: a buyback of this size supports the downside, but it is a floor statement, not a growth statement.

This is a move to neutral, not a bear call. The core franchise is holding — top accounts keep growing above the company average, and management described the first quarter as the likely trough of the cycle. Patience may still be rewarded in this story; at this point, however, we prefer to exercise it from the sidelines. What would bring us back to a positive stance: the second-half contracts converting into a visible growth inflection, AI Pods tracking toward the upper half of the year-end target, and a stable Middle East. Until growth shows up in the P&L, the risk/reward no longer supports a positive rating.

CI&T: reiterating Outperform

Why we remain constructive. Our thesis on CI&T has always been about execution and differentiation — a company using its proprietary platform to win work, gain share and protect pricing through a cycle that is punishing the rest of the sector. Each quarter has added evidence rather than doubt: 1Q26 marked the sixth consecutive quarter of double-digit organic growth, the full-year outlook was raised on the back of demand rather than currency, and, most importantly for the medium term, the quality of growth keeps improving — broader across clients, verticals and regions, and increasingly monetized under new pricing models. In this context, we reiterate our Outperform rating; CINT remains our preferred pick in IT services.

The operational momentum keeps compounding. Net revenue rose 23.2% YoY in 1Q26 (+15.5% FX-neutral, fully organic), once again above the company's guidance and our estimates. More telling than the print was the follow-through: the FY26 outlook was raised to 13.5–17.5% organic growth, with most of the revision attributed to demand and pipeline conversion, while the 2Q26 floor implies close to 20% YoY growth. The pattern is familiar by now — conservative guidance, delivery above it, upward revision — and management signaled its intention to keep repeating it as the pipeline converts. Considering that pipeline is up ~30% YoY and entirely related to AI deployment, we see the bias to the numbers still pointing up.

Growth has rotated from one client to the whole book. The old bear case on CI&T was concentration — a growth story carried by a single large account. The 1Q26 cohort data dismantles that argument. The top client is decelerating naturally as it matures, exactly as expected; what matters is what happened around it: revenue excluding the top client accelerated from roughly flat a year ago to +23% YoY, the fastest pace in the series, and the ex-top-10 book — still a drag in early 2025 — is now the largest contributor to consolidated growth. The middle of the pyramid is thickening too, with the US$ 5–10 million client bracket expanding from 15 to 18 accounts in a single quarter. In our view, this is what de-risked, broad-based demand looks like — and it is the mirror image of Globant, where growth is narrowing into the top accounts. Monetization has moved from promise to evidence. We have long argued that Flow would let CI&T capture the productivity gain from AI rather than hand it back to clients; this was the quarter that view became measurable. One-fifth of new sales were already signed under new pricing models — output-, consumption- and outcome-based — and management quantified the economics for the first time, pointing to a contribution-margin uplift of 3 to 15 percentage points per engagement. Just as important, the transmission to the P&L is mapped: the contract base renews over roughly 18 months, with gross-margin gains expected to become visible already from 2Q26. The pricing environment was described as rational, with clients open to repricing even legacy T&M work around AI capabilities. In our view, this is the key indicator of the sustainability of the operating trends ahead — and it is our sector deflation thesis running in reverse.

The talent equation closes the loop. A platform-led model only compounds if delivery keeps up, and here the signals are equally supportive: voluntary attrition of ~10% is the lowest in the company's recent history and the best in the peer group, headcount keeps growing below FX-neutral revenue — positive jaws that should widen as Flow scales — and most of the workforce already operates as AI builders. Rather than shrinking the team, management treats productivity as an expansion of the addressable opportunity, and keeps hiring AI-native junior talent, which also cushions FX pressure on gross margin.

Margins are the swing factor, not the thesis. The 1Q26 adjusted EBITDA margin of 15.2% came in below consensus, pressured by BRL appreciation, higher Brazilian payroll taxes and typical first-quarter seasonality; the 17–19% full-year guidance is unchanged and requires a second-half ramp. Two nuances keep us comfortable. First, the FX drag should attenuate through the year, and the FX-neutral margin already stands at 17.4%. Second, management intends to reinvest the incremental gross margin from new pricing models into go-to-market rather than let it flow to EBITDA — a deliberate trade of near-term margin surprise for growth durability, which we endorse. If value-based pricing scales as mapped, margin becomes an upside risk over the repricing cycle.

Risks are identifiable — and none is thesis-breaking. Consumer Goods was the only vertical described as flat; the guidance implies some constant-currency deceleration in the second half, which we read as the same conservatism that has preceded six consecutive beats; FX flatters reported growth; and the top client still represents ~11% of revenues. On the macro front that dominates the Globant debate, the contrast is clean: management sees no meaningful impact from the conflict, and the company carries no relevant Middle East or travel exposure. Trading at 10.3x/9.3x non-GAAP P/E for 2026/27 — still a ~45% discount to its own history — while raising guidance as peers cut, we reiterate our Outperform rating and YE26 target price of US$ 7.00/share.

The same wave, two distances from monetization. Both earnings calls confirmed the same structural point: clients accept output- and consumption-based pricing, meaning AI deflation can be converted into price rather than surrendered. The difference lies in where each company stands on that road. At Globant, AI monetization is still an early-stage product line — roughly 1% of revenue — defended with a buyback while the traditional book stabilizes. At CI&T, it is already embedded in the commercial engine and set to reprice the entire contract base within 18 months. That distance, in our view, is the decoupling of operational momentum in one sentence — and it is why we now rate the two names apart.
